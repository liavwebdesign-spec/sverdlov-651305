Objecting to a Will: When You Can Object and How It Works

Who may object to a will, the four main grounds, the deadline for filing, what happens once an objection is lodged, and why home-made wills are vulnerable.

Not everyone disappointed by the contents of a will can have it set aside. There are specific legal grounds that have to be proved. That said, where the grounds genuinely exist, anyone with an interest in the estate has the right to file a formal objection.

Who May File an Objection

Anyone with a genuine interest in the estate: an heir under the law, a person who was a beneficiary under an earlier will, or any other interested party. The objection is filed within the window that opens when the public notice of the application for the order is published.

The Main Grounds for Objection

Lack of capacity: the testator was not legally competent to make a will at the time it was made, for example because of their cognitive condition at that time

Undue influence: someone exploited their position, or the testator's dependence on them, in order to influence the contents of the will improperly

Fraud or forgery: the will, or parts of it, do not reflect the testator's true wishes

A defect in the process of making the will: failure to meet the formal requirements of the relevant type of will, for example incompetent witnesses to a will before witnesses

What Happens Once an Objection Is Filed

The moment a formal objection is filed with the Registrar of Inheritance Affairs, the Registrar loses the power to decide. The entire file, both the original application and the objection, is transferred to the Family Court, where it proceeds as a full-fledged action, including the hearing of evidence and witnesses.

A Person Who Took Part in Making the Will, and Their Spouse

This is one of the points on which home-made wills most often fail, and it is broader than people tend to think. A provision in a will that benefits the person who drafted it, a person who witnessed its making, or a person who otherwise took part in making it, and likewise a provision benefiting the spouse of any of these, is void. In other words, it is not only a witness who benefits under the will: a son who typed up the will for his father and left something in it to his wife falls within the rule as well. The defective provision is void, and usually the rest of the will remains in force. The rule does not apply to an oral will.
