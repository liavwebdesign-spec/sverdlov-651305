Small Claims Court Versus an Ordinary Civil Claim: Which Track Suits You

The monetary ceiling, who may sue, how many claims a year are allowed, when a lawyer may represent you, and when the ordinary civil track still fits better.

A monetary dispute does not always require full civil proceedings. The Small Claims Court (beit mishpat le-tvi'ot ktanot) is a shorter and simpler track, but it does not suit every situation, and it has a number of limitations worth knowing about before you file.

What a Small Claim Actually Is

The Small Claims Court hears monetary claims up to a ceiling that is updated annually, and as of September 2026 stands at approximately NIS 39,900. Its jurisdiction is limited to a monetary claim, or a claim for the replacement or repair of goods, and does not extend to every cause of action. The process is relatively simple and quick: there are no strict formal rules of evidence, you can file a claim on your own without representation, and the judge may, with the parties' consent, rule by way of compromise rather than strictly according to law.

Who May File a Small Claim

This point surprises many people. As a claimant, the track is open only to private individuals, including a licensed self-employed person suing in their personal name. Companies and corporations generally cannot file claims there as claimants, though they can certainly be sued there as defendants. There is also a quantity limit: a person may file up to five small claims in the same court in the same year.

Representation by a Lawyer

In the Small Claims Court the parties are generally not represented by lawyers, because the purpose of the track is to allow an ordinary person to run the case themselves. Representation is possible only with the court's permission and for special reasons to be recorded, and there is no closed list of such cases. One circumstance accepted in the case law is where the other side is represented and an imbalance arises. Note that a lawyer who is themselves a party to the case is not considered represented, but appears in their own name.

When the Ordinary Track Is Still Preferable

Even where the amount falls within the small claims range, there are situations in which the ordinary civil track is preferable: where the case is legally complex and requires an expert opinion, where there is a genuine need for professional legal representation throughout the proceedings, or where a counterclaim exceeding the monetary ceiling is expected.
