Limitation Periods in Civil Claims: How Long You Have to File

Seven years as a rule, 15 or 25 years for land, the exception for minors, and the late discovery rule with its ten-year cap. What starts the clock running.

One of the first questions worth clarifying before planning a civil claim is how long you actually have to file it. The Limitation Law, 5718-1958, sets different time frames for different situations, and missing them can bar a fully justified claim.

The General Rule: Seven Years

In most civil claims, including contract claims and tort claims, the limitation period is seven years from the date the cause of action arose, which is usually the date of the breach or the date of the loss. It is important to understand that limitation is a procedural bar rather than the extinction of the right itself, but in practice, if the other side raises a limitation defense and the court accepts it, the claim is dismissed.

Land: Two Periods, Not One

In claims concerning land the Law sets two different periods, and many people know only one of them. For settled land, meaning land registered following a settlement of title process, the period is 25 years. For unsettled land the period is 15 years. Be precise about the terminology: what matters is whether the land has gone through settlement of title, not the mere fact that it is registered at the Land Registry (Tabu). Assuming that "registered at the Tabu" automatically means 25 years can prove expensive.

Minors: The Clock Does Not Run Until Age 18

Where the potential claimant is a minor, the limitation period does not begin to run until they reach majority. In practice, someone injured as a child has a full seven years starting from their 18th birthday, meaning until the age of 25, even if many years have passed since the event.

When the Loss Comes to Light Later: The Discovery Rule

There are situations in which the loss only comes to light years after the event that caused it. In tort claims concerning damage, the limitation period starts running from the day the damage was discovered, or from the day it could have been discovered with reasonable diligence. But this rule carries a cut-off: as a rule, no claim may be brought once ten years have passed from the date of the event that caused the damage. The discovery rule is not an unlimited safety net, and that is a common mistake.

Why It Is Still Better Not to Wait

Even where there appears to be plenty of time left, a long delay hurts your practical prospects of success: witnesses forget details, documents go missing, and evidence becomes harder to gather. The limitation period is a legal outer limit, not a recommendation about when to act.
