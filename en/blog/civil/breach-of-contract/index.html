Breach of Contract: Your Rights and the Remedies You Can Demand

The three remedies for breach of contract, what makes a breach fundamental, why notice of rescission must be timely, and when damages need no proof of loss.

When a party to a contract fails to meet its obligations, the Contracts (Remedies for Breach of Contract) Law, 5731-1970, gives the injured party three main remedies: enforcement, rescission and damages. They do not necessarily conflict with one another, and under certain conditions they can be combined.

Enforcement: Compelling the Other Side to Perform the Contract

Enforcement is the default remedy. The injured party can ask the court to order the party in breach actually to do what it undertook to do. Section 3 of the Law lists four situations in which the court will refuse to order enforcement: where the contract is incapable of performance; where it involves personal work or a personal service; where enforcement would require an unreasonable degree of supervision by the court; and where enforcement would be unjust in the circumstances.

Rescinding the Contract, and the Notice You Must Not Forget

Where the breach is fundamental, the injured party may rescind the contract without granting an extension to cure it. Where the breach is not fundamental, the injured party must first allow a reasonable extension to cure the breach, and may rescind only if it is not cured. But in both cases there is a requirement many people miss: rescission is effected by a written notice of rescission, which must be delivered within a reasonable time from the moment the injured party learned of the breach. Unreasonable delay may extinguish the right of rescission altogether and be read as a waiver of the breach. Put differently, a fundamental breach entitles you to rescind immediately in the sense that no extension is required, but it does not rescind the contract by itself.

What Happens After Rescission

Rescission gives rise to a mutual duty of restitution: each side returns what it received. Where restitution in kind is impossible or unreasonable, the value of what was received is paid instead. It is important to know that rescission does not erase the contract retroactively for all purposes: certain clauses, such as an arbitration clause, an agreed damages clause, confidentiality or choice of law, continue to apply after it.

Damages, and When You Do Not Have to Prove Loss

Damages may be claimed in addition to enforcement or in addition to rescission. As a rule you must prove loss that was foreseeable at the time the contract was made as a probable consequence of the breach. There are several routes that do not require full proof of loss: agreed damages fixed in advance in the contract; compensation in the amount of the difference between the contract price and the value of the asset or service on the date of rescission, where the contract has been rescinded; and compensation for non-pecuniary loss, meaning distress and aggravation, which the court may award in an amount it sees fit in the circumstances.

What to Do in Real Time

As soon as you identify a breach, document it in writing, by email or message, rather than settling for a conversation. It is also worth considering sending a formal warning that gives the other side an opportunity to cure. Even if you ultimately choose rescission or litigation, that documentation is very important for proving the sequence of events, and it is also what shows that you acted within a reasonable time.
