The Caveat (He'arat Azhara): What It Is, and Why It Is Your Most Important Protection in a Real Estate Transaction

What a caveat in the Israeli land register does, what it protects a buyer against, when it cannot be recorded at all, and which mechanisms replace it.

A caveat, or priority notice (he'arat azhara), is an entry in the land register announcing to the public that the holder of rights in the property has undertaken, in a written agreement, to carry out a particular transaction in the property or to refrain from doing so. The notice itself is not a transaction and does not transfer ownership. Its function is to prevent the registration of a conflicting transaction without the consent of the person in whose favor it was recorded, or without a court order.

Why It Matters So Much

A contract binds the parties to it, but it does not necessarily protect against a third party. Without a caveat, a seller who has signed with one buyer can sell the same land to a second buyer, and the seller's creditors can try to attach the property. It is the registration of the caveat that creates that protection.

And What Happens If the Seller Becomes Insolvent

Section 127(b) of the Land Law gives a caveat protection even when an order to open proceedings is issued against the seller, but the protection is neither automatic nor absolute. It is limited to the rights the caveat was recorded to secure, and the case law also examines good faith and whether consideration was actually paid. In addition, a trustee in insolvency proceedings may seek to set a transaction aside as a gift or as a preference of creditors. In other words, a caveat greatly improves the buyer's position, but it is no substitute for a proper review of the seller's financial condition before signing.

A Common Mistake

Many buyers think that once they have signed the contract they are protected. That is not accurate. What protects you against a third party is the registration of the caveat, not the signing of the agreement. One of the first things worth confirming in any real estate transaction is therefore that the caveat is actually recorded, as soon as possible after signing, rather than settling for a promise that it will be recorded shortly.

When a Caveat Cannot Be Recorded at All

In a substantial share of land transactions in Israel a caveat cannot be recorded in the land registers at all: land administered by the Israel Land Authority (Rama'i), assets managed through a housing corporation, and land that has not yet gone through settlement of title. In such cases there are alternative protective mechanisms, including recording a notice in the Israel Land Authority's own registers, notifying the housing corporation, an irrevocable power of attorney, and registering a pledge with the Registrar of Pledges. The right combination depends on the type of asset, which is one more reason to establish early on which register the rights are held in at all.

When the Seller Objects or Drags Their Feet

A seller who hesitates to record a caveat, or tries to postpone the registration to a later stage, is a red flag. In a sound transaction the registration is a technical, procedural step, and there is no genuine reason to delay it. Repeated hesitation on this point justifies taking another look at the rest of the transaction.

FAQ

FAQ

Since the online registration process of the Land Registration and Settlement Department came into operation, a lawyer can record a caveat online, and in many cases the registration is completed on the same business day. That assumes there is no problem with the property's existing registration, such as an unresolved encumbrance, and that the property is registered in the land registers.

No. A caveat protects against future transactions that attempt to be registered after it. If a conflicting right was already registered before your caveat was recorded, the caveat does not cancel it retroactively. That is exactly why an up-to-date Land Registry extract and prompt registration are so critical.

A caveat is interim protection and it does not make you the owner. Full registration of ownership takes place at a later stage, usually after payment has been completed and all the conditions of the agreement have been met, and that is the point at which your right in the property becomes final and complete.
