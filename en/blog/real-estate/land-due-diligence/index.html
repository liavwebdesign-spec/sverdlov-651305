How to Check a Plot of Land Before You Buy It: The Complete Guide

What to check before buying land in Israel: the registry extract, planning designation, expropriation risk, the seller's standing and the deal documents.

Buying land is fundamentally different from buying an apartment. When you buy a second-hand apartment the picture is usually reasonably clear: one Land Registry extract (nesach tabu), a house committee, neighbors you can ask. With land there is no ready-made picture. You have to build it yourself, and each check answers a different question. Something missed in one check will not necessarily surface in another, and that is precisely why due diligence on land is a structured process rather than a single step.

Ownership and Encumbrances: What the Land Registry Extract Shows

The first check is an up-to-date registry extract, which shows who the registered owner is and what encumbrances, mortgages or caveats (he'arot azhara) are recorded against the land. It is important to distinguish between settled land, registered in the Register of Rights, and unsettled land, registered only in the Register of Deeds. In the Register of Rights, registration is conclusive evidence of its contents (section 125(a) of the Land Law), although even it can be corrected in exceptional cases of fraud or error. In the Register of Deeds, registration is prima facie evidence only, so unsettled land generally calls for an examination of the historical chain of title rather than reliance on the existing registration alone.

Planning Status: What You Are Actually Allowed to Build

A clean Land Registry extract says nothing about what may be built on the land. That is checked with the local or district planning and building committee and through the Planning Administration's "Tochnit Zamin" planning information system, by block and parcel (gush ve-chelka): what the existing designation is (residential, agricultural, commercial and so on), what the building rights actually are, and which zoning plans (ta'ba) apply to the area. This is usually the check that has the greatest bearing on the true economic value of the land.

Expropriation: The Risk Most Buyers Miss

Section 190(a)(1) of the Planning and Building Law allows the local committee to expropriate up to 40% of a parcel for public purposes without paying compensation. The practical implication is significant: a plot that looks a certain size on paper may shrink considerably in reality, and its value falls accordingly. A planning check should therefore cover not only what may be built, but also whether the applicable plans designate part of the land for a road, an open public space or a public building, and whether an expropriation has already been carried out on that parcel in the past.

The Seller's Stability, Especially When the Seller Is a Company

When the seller is a company rather than an individual, one more check is added: an up-to-date company extract from the Registrar of Companies, and a check of whether insolvency proceedings are pending against the company. A transaction with a company in financial difficulty can become complicated even if the land itself is entirely clean.

Not All Land Is Registered at the Land Registry

A substantial share of the land in Israel is not registered in the land registers in the name of the person holding it. State land is managed by the Israel Land Authority (Rama'i, commonly called the Minhal), certain assets are administered through a housing corporation, and some land has not yet gone through the settlement of title process. In each of these situations the check is carried out with the managing body and not only with the Land Registry office, and the ways of protecting the buyer are different as well. This is worth clarifying early, because it changes the entire way the transaction is handled.

The Transaction Documents Themselves

Finally, you check not only the land but also the transaction documents: earlier sale agreements relating to the same land, powers of attorney, and contractual conditions set in previous purchase transactions. The aim is to confirm that the documents do not contradict one another, and that there is no earlier undertaking that is not reflected in the registration.

You Should Check Whether There Is a Co-Ownership Agreement

Where land is held in co-ownership, the ownership itself does not regulate who uses what, who bears the expenses or how decisions are taken. That is the job of a co-ownership agreement (heskem shituf), which sets out the division of use between the owners, the rights and obligations of each of them, and the mechanism for advancing planning proceedings on the land. Without one, every planning step requires fresh consent from all of the co-owners, and any co-owner is entitled to demand dissolution of the co-ownership at any time. It is important that the agreement be registered at the Land Registry office, because only a registered agreement also binds anyone who acquires a share in the land in the future.

What to Do If the Check Turns Up a Problem

Not every problem that comes to light disqualifies the transaction. Sometimes it is a point that can be fixed before signing, such as completing a missing registration. Sometimes it is a basis for negotiating the price, and sometimes it is a genuine reason to walk away. The central value of the check is that it happens before signing. Once you have signed and made the first payment, your room to maneuver narrows considerably.
