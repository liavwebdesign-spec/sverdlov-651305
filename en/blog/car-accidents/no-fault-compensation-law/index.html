The Road Accident Victims Compensation Law: Why You Usually Do Not Need to Prove Fault

Absolute liability under the Piltad law: whom you sue, why contributory negligence does not apply, what is compensated, the exceptions, and Karnit's role.

One of the things that most surprises people injured in a road accident is that in most cases there is no need to prove that anyone was negligent. The Road Accident Victims Compensation Law, 5735-1975 (known in Israel by its acronym, Piltad), is built on absolute liability: the duty to compensate falls on the person using the vehicle and on their insurer, regardless of the question of fault.

Whom You Actually Sue

This is the first practical point, and many people get it wrong. Under section 3 of the Law, a driver and passengers generally sue the insurer of the vehicle they were traveling in, not the other driver or that driver's insurance company. An injured pedestrian sues the insurer of the vehicle that struck them. This means that even in an accident where you are convinced the other side was at fault, your claim will usually be conducted against your own insurer, and that does not affect your entitlement.

No Contributory Negligence

Unlike ordinary tort law, the Road Accident Victims Compensation Law does not reduce compensation on account of the victim's contributory negligence. Even a victim who was negligent receives full compensation, apart from the exceptional cases listed in the Law, chief among them causing the accident deliberately.

Who Is Covered, and How It Is Funded

The Law applies to a person injured as a user of a vehicle, as a passenger or as a pedestrian struck by a vehicle, and also to the dependents of a victim who has died. The funding comes from the compulsory insurance that every vehicle owner is required to purchase.

What Counts as a "Road Accident"

Under the statutory definition, a road accident is an event in which bodily injury is caused to a person as a result of the use of a motor vehicle for transport purposes, including getting into or out of the vehicle, roadside servicing and further cases.

What Is Compensated

The main heads of damage are past loss of earnings, future loss of earnings, medical expenses, third-party assistance, pain and suffering, and support for dependents where the victim has died. It is important to know that compensation under the Road Accident Victims Compensation Law is not unlimited. The law and its regulations set ceilings and calculation mechanisms for some heads of damage, including pain and suffering and the earnings base used to calculate loss of earnings. This means the actual amount of compensation follows those rules and not only the extent of the loss, so each case should be examined on its own facts.

Karnit: When There Is No One to Recover From

Where there is no insurance company from which the compensation can be recovered, for example a pedestrian struck in a hit-and-run or by an uninsured vehicle, the Compensation Fund for Road Accident Victims, known as Karnit, comes into the picture.

The Cases Where the Law Does Not Apply

Absolute liability is not unlimited. Section 7 of the Law denies compensation to a person who caused the accident deliberately, to a person who drove without a valid driving license, to a person who used the vehicle without the owner's permission, and to a person for whom the vehicle served or assisted in committing a felony. The two exceptions that catch the most people are actually the less familiar ones: a person who drove a vehicle without insurance or where the insurance they held did not cover their use of the vehicle, and an owner or holder of a vehicle who allowed someone else to drive it without insurance and was injured in that same drive. A policy that does not cover a young driver below the age specified in it, or an undeclared commercial use, is exactly the situation in which a person discovers after the fact that they were not covered. Each case must be examined on its own facts.

The Trade-off That Comes With Absolute Liability

Alongside the great advantage of compensation without proof of fault there is also a price, and it is worth knowing about: section 8 of the Law establishes exclusivity of cause of action. A person who has a cause of action under this Law cannot bring a parallel claim for the same bodily injury under the Torts Ordinance. That is why the definition of what counts as a road accident is not a technical question but one that determines the track along which the entire claim will run.
